Scholarly article title: Customizing Cloud‑Native Systems for the Manufacturing Sector

Abstract:
In the contemporary business environment, manufacturing enterprises face ever‑higher demands for efficiency and adaptability. The adoption of cloud services has emerged as an effective approach for optimizing communication within organizations, maintaining relationships with business clients and partners, and meeting rapidly changing market expectations. The topic’s relevance is driven by the trend of cloud technologies becoming a strategic tool for future growth, modernization, and digital transformation within manufacturing enterprises. This trend is likely to continue as cloud platforms enable the rapid implementation of innovative ideas.

Introduction:
In the context of the Fourth Industrial Revolution, characterized by the widespread application of digital technologies in manufacturing, several challenges have arisen:
- the need for interoperability and interaction among various internal and external information systems;
- the need to ensure cybersecurity and reliable data‑exchange mechanisms;
- the need for dependable storage and processing of large volumes of data;
- compliance with local regulations in global operations.

The development of cloud services, particularly the IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS models, opens new opportunities for the digitalization of business processes in the manufacturing sector. Applying these models eliminates the need to maintain local computer infrastructure typical of on‑premises services, while providing companies with the ability to implement software products tailored to their specific needs. The flexibility of these models allows enterprises to adapt to changing market conditions by facilitating faster decision‑making based on real‑time data and by improving planning and forecasting capabilities.

The purpose of this study is to analyze and design an integrated cloud‑native architecture that unifies complex manufacturing business operations by configuring IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS components to enterprise‑specific requirements. A review of scholarly and industry sources reveals a lack of a well‑defined conceptual model that holistically addresses manufacturing complexity through integrated cloud capabilities. Consequently, the subject of this research is a high‑level conceptual model for a distributed manufacturing information system.

Research methodology:
The study’s methodological framework employed several methods: a systematic literature review of scholarly articles and white papers; comparative analysis of leading platforms, web, and mobile technologies; classification and typification to group manufacturing information systems and map them to business capabilities; and modeling of the target cloud‑native architecture using graphical, schematic, and pictorial approaches.

HEADLINE 1 – Opportunities for Customizing Business Processes Through Cloud Technologies

Historically, manufacturing enterprises have relied on manual processes involving numerous participants across various departments and external partners. In the late 1990s, a range of business systems was introduced, including Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP), Customer Relationship Management (CRM), Manufacturing Execution Systems (MES), Supply Chain Management (SCM), Product Lifecycle Management (PLM), Enterprise Asset Management (EAM), Quality Management Systems (QMS), Advanced Planning and Scheduling (APS), and Supplier Relationship Management (SRM). However, these systems often function as standalone tools, limiting communication and information sharing among internal and external stakeholders. This lack of integration hinders efficient data entry and processing and creates significant barriers to effective business‑process management.

According to various studies, ERP functions as the central hub, connecting strategic planning, procurement, operations, logistics, and the product lifecycle. A corporate system such as SAP S/4HANA often forms the core of an interconnected model. Generally, SAP stands out as one of the leading ERP solutions. As of April 2025, 86% of Fortune 500 companies and 92% of Forbes Global 2000 companies relied on SAP. More than 77% of global revenue transactions are processed through this system, which is used by over 400,000 organizations across 180 countries. SAP’s capabilities are divided into modules organized under three main categories: logistics, accounting, and human resources management.

Additional subsystems that can complement SAP’s capabilities include Oracle NetSuite, JD Edwards EnterpriseOne, Microsoft Dynamics 365, and Infor CloudSuite. These systems manage the flow of goods, materials, information, and capital, helping to ensure effective and coordinated operations. Together, they contribute to an interconnected model—presented in Figure 1—aimed at developing a cohesive strategy for delivering finished products.

This interconnected model enhances quality and cost efficiency, which are essential for modern manufacturing environments. Organizations can improve forecasting, resource allocation, inventory management, and coordination across procurement, production operations, and logistics. However, interactions among these internal and external information subsystems require resilience, high availability, accessibility, scalability, and manageability.

Building on this interconnected model, the next stage involves customizing a cloud‑native system. The aim of the cloud system is to function as a central integrator, connecting diverse subsystems such as ERP, CRM, MES, and SCM. This model ensures continuous data exchange. HEADLINE 2 – Architecture of a Cloud‑Based Manufacturing Information System

This chapter examines the system’s core components, exploring their interrelationships and overall composition. Configured to the specific needs of a manufacturing enterprise, the cloud system manages key business processes and activities by extracting and analyzing data in real time. Implementing the conceptual model proceeds through an iterative process involving two stages: defining the functional and non‑functional requirements and performing a high‑level concept review.

According to Vasilev, well‑defined requirements are crucial to a project’s success. By examining the theoretical foundations, terminology, and technologies, both functional and non‑functional requirements can be established. The system’s functional requirements focus on enhancing business planning, control, management, data analytics, document workflows, user administration, and integration capabilities through application programming interfaces (APIs). These capabilities should be supported across web, desktop, and mobile applications, enabling direct use by business customers, partners, and stakeholders. Furthermore, the system could provide real‑time data through Internet of Things (IoT) devices, ensuring timely and accurate information.

Common non‑functional requirements include ensuring continuous system operation with minimal downtime; providing scalable capacity by adding or adjusting resources to accommodate increased workloads; maintaining correct operation despite individual component failures; meeting responsiveness and latency targets under anticipated loads; and offering comprehensive insights into system health through metrics, logs, and traces.

Based on the defined requirements and business operations, the conceptual model (Figure 3) encompasses client applications, cloud microservices, and internal and external corporate subsystems.

(Figure 3. High‑level conceptual model (author’s development).)

The cloud‑based system is composed of autonomous modules, each with distinct responsibilities. These modules aggregate and store data through a suite of microservices and NoSQL databases that integrate with internal subsystems and external IoT devices. The services that support these modules adhere to the principles and practices of IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS. IaaS resources orchestrate virtual machines, private networks, and load balancers. The microservices and databases rely on PaaS, while SaaS supports streaming, monitoring, and message‑broker backing services.

A comparative analysis of web‑based frameworks and programming languages shows that ASP.NET Core is a suitable choice for building microservices. Further evaluation of key technological aspects reveals that Microsoft Azure has established itself as a leading cloud platform.

The following summarizes the technological tools selected for implementing the cloud system:
• ASP.NET Core for server‑side logic and microservices
• Azure as the public cloud service provider
• Azure SQL Database, Cosmos DB, and Blob Storage for data storage
• RabbitMQ as the message broker
• Blazor for the web portal
• .NET MAUI for mobile application development
